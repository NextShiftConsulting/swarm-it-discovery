# Measuring the Fragility of Trust: Devising Credibility Index via Explanation Stability (CIES) for Business Decision Support Systems

## Core Contribution

This paper tackles the important challenge of measuring the **fragility of trust** in business decision support systems (DSS). As AI and machine learning models become increasingly integrated into high-stakes decision-making processes, understanding the **reliability and trustworthiness** of these systems is crucial. The authors' key innovation is the development of a **Credibility Index via Explanation Stability (CIES)**, a novel metric that quantifies the stability and consistency of model explanations as a proxy for the system's overall credibility.

The core insight is that stable, consistent explanations are a critical component of **trustworthy AI**. Fluctuations or noise in the explanations provided by a DSS can undermine the human user's confidence in the system, even if the overall predictions are accurate. By measuring this **explanation stability**, the CIES can serve as an informative signal of a model's **fragility of trust** - a crucial consideration for real-world business applications where reliable decision support is paramount.

## Technical Approach

The authors propose a three-step methodology for computing the CIES metric:

1. **Explanation Generation**: They use a state-of-the-art **explainable AI** technique, such as SHAP or LIME, to generate feature importance explanations for the model's predictions.

2. **Explanation Perturbation**: They introduce **controlled perturbations** to the input data, capturing the impact of **input noise** on the model's explanations. This could involve adding small amounts of Gaussian noise or performing targeted feature manipulations.

3. **Stability Quantification**: The authors then measure the **consistency** of the perturbed explanations compared to the original, unperturbed explanations. They use a combination of cosine similarity and L1 distance to compute the CIES score, which ranges from 0 (unstable) to 1 (stable).

Importantly, the authors evaluate their CIES metric on a diverse set of **real-world business datasets**, including credit risk assessment, customer churn prediction, and fraud detection. This ensures the proposed approach is grounded in practical, high-stakes decision-making scenarios.

## Key Results

The authors' experiments demonstrate that the CIES metric is able to effectively capture the **fragility of trust** in various business DSS models. Key findings include:

- The CIES scores were able to differentiate between **robust** and **fragile** models, aligning with human evaluations of trustworthiness.
- Models with higher CIES scores (i.e., more stable explanations) also exhibited **better out-of-sample performance**, suggesting a connection between explanation stability and overall system reliability.
- The CIES metric was found to be more informative than **existing trust measures**, such as model accuracy or feature importance statistics, in identifying potentially **unreliable** decision support systems.

These results highlight the value of the CIES approach in providing a **quantitative, explanatory signal** of a model's trustworthiness, beyond traditional performance metrics.

## Significance & Limitations

The significance of this work lies in its potential to **enhance the transparency and accountability** of business decision support systems. By empowering practitioners to assess the **fragility of trust** in their AI models, the CIES metric can inform model selection, deployment, and ongoing monitoring. This is especially important in high-stakes domains where **reliability and explainability** are critical.

That said, the authors acknowledge several limitations and future research directions. First, the CIES metric focuses solely on **explanation stability**, and does not capture other important dimensions of trust, such as **model accuracy, fairness, or robustness**. Integrating these complementary signals could provide a more holistic assessment of trustworthiness.

Additionally, the current implementation relies on **post-hoc explanations** generated by existing techniques (e.g., SHAP, LIME). Exploring methods to **directly optimize for explanation stability** during model training could yield further improvements. Finally, the authors suggest exploring the **generalization** of the CIES approach to other types of AI systems, beyond business decision support.
