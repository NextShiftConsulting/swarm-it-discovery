# Agent Role Structure and Operating Characteristics in Large Language Model Clinical Classification: A Comparative Study of Specialist and Deliberative Multi-Agent Protocols

**RSCT Certification:** κ=0.550 (pending) | **RSN:** 0.38/0.32/0.31 | **Topics:** llm-agents

**Core Contribution**

This paper presents a comparative study of two deterministic multi-agent protocols for clinical classification tasks using large language models (LLMs). The key innovation is the controlled isolation of the effects of internal role decomposition within the multi-agent architecture, as opposed to confounding factors like changes in model configuration, training, or decoding. By evaluating the **Generic Deliberative (GD)** and **Feature-Specialist (FS)** protocols under identical base model weights, decoding settings, and computational budgets, the authors are able to demonstrate that role structure alone can systematically reshape the operating characteristics of the LLM-based decision system.

This is an important contribution because multi-agent prompting architectures are increasingly being deployed in safety-sensitive domains like healthcare, where the ability to control sensitivity-specificity trade-offs is crucial. Prior work has often conflated workflow structure with other model changes, obscuring the specific effects of role decomposition. By disentangling these factors, this paper provides a clearer understanding of how multi-agent protocols can be leveraged as an explicit mechanism for adjusting error distributions in LLM-powered clinical decision support.

**Technical Approach**

The authors evaluated two multi-agent protocols, GD and FS, on two tabular clinical benchmarks: the **UCI Cleveland Heart Disease** dataset and the **Pima Indians Diabetes** dataset. The GD protocol uses a deliberative approach, where agents with distinct roles (e.g., symptom assessment, risk factor analysis, treatment recommendation) collaborate to reach a final decision. In contrast, the FS protocol employs a more specialized division of labor, with agents focusing on specific feature subsets (e.g., demographics, lab results, medical history).

Crucially, the base LLM parameters, decoding settings, computational budget, and adjudication logic were held constant across the two multi-agent architectures. This allows the authors to isolate the effects of role structure as the sole manipulated variable. The experiments were run using a proprietary LLM-based clinical reasoning system, with details about the specific model family and training data omitted.

**Key Results**

On the Cleveland Heart Disease dataset, the FS protocol outperformed the GD protocol, improving accuracy by 0.07 and macro-F1 by 0.06. However, this improvement came at the cost of a substantial shift in the operating characteristics, with FS exhibiting higher specificity (+0.22) and lower sensitivity (-0.13) compared to GD. This suggests that the FS architecture was more effective at reducing false positives, but at the expense of increased false negatives.

In contrast, on the Pima Indians Diabetes dataset, the results were reversed: the GD protocol achieved the strongest macro performance, with an accuracy of 0.68 and macro-F1 of 0.64. The FS protocol, on the other hand, induced a pronounced class asymmetry, with a recall of 0.95 for the positive class but only 0.27 for the negative class.

**Significance & Limitations**

These findings demonstrate that internal role decomposition within multi-agent LLM architectures can function as a structured inductive bias, materially altering the error distributions without changes to the base model parameters. This has important implications for the deployment of LLMs in safety-critical domains, where the ability to control sensitivity-specificity trade-offs is essential.

However, the paper also highlights the potential for context-dependence in the effects of role structure. The reversal of results between the two datasets suggests that the optimal multi-agent protocol may depend on the specific characteristics of the problem domain and dataset. Additionally, the use of a proprietary LLM system limits the generalizability of the findings, and future work should explore the robustness of these effects across a wider range of LLM architectures and clinical benchmarks.
