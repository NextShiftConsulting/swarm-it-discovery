# Do Foundation Models Know Geometry? Probing Frozen Features for Continuous Physical Measurement

**RSCT Certification:** κ=0.494 (pending) | **RSN:** 0.37/0.32/0.31 | **Topics:** representation-learning

Title: Unlocking Geometric Knowledge in Foundation Models: A Comprehensive Review

**Core Contribution**

This paper presents a groundbreaking exploration of the geometric knowledge encoded within vision-language foundation models. The key finding is that these models contain a wealth of continuous geometric information in their visual pathways, which is not fully expressed in their text-based outputs. The authors demonstrate that a simple linear probe can extract hand joint angles with remarkable accuracy (6.1 degrees mean absolute error) directly from the frozen features of these models, far outperforming the text-based predictions (20.0 degrees). This represents a significant bottleneck in the models' ability to convey geometric understanding through language.

The importance of this work lies in its implications for the development of AI systems that can seamlessly integrate and leverage continuous physical and spatial knowledge. By uncovering the hidden geometric knowledge within foundation models, the authors pave the way for unlocking new capabilities in areas such as robotics, human-computer interaction, and embodied AI, where the ability to accurately and efficiently represent and reason about the physical world is crucial.

**Technical Approach**

The authors adopt a multi-pronged approach to investigate the geometric knowledge in foundation models. First, they train a 6,000-parameter linear probe to extract hand joint angles from the frozen features of various vision-language models, including self-supervised, contrastive, and hybrid architectures. This probe significantly outperforms the text-based predictions of the same models, demonstrating a clear gap between the visual and linguistic representations.

To further explore this gap, the authors fine-tune the models using a lightweight LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) technique, which narrows the accuracy difference between the visual and text-based predictions. This suggests that the observed bottleneck is not due to a representational deficit, but rather a deficit in the training objective and pathway.

The authors also analyze the impact of different training objectives on the models' geometric knowledge. They find that despite having as little as 0.41 representational similarity, five encoders spanning diverse training paradigms converge to statistically equivalent geometric prediction accuracy. This points to a functional convergence in the models' ability to encode continuous physical information, irrespective of their architectural or representational differences.

Additionally, the authors investigate the impact of autoregressive text generation on geometric fidelity, revealing that the damage originates in the generation process itself, rather than in the language alignment. Interestingly, they find that the language model layers in the Qwen2.5-VL architecture actually improve the geometric probe accuracy compared to the raw vision encoder.

**Key Results**

The key findings of this study can be summarized as follows:

- A 6,000-parameter linear probe can extract hand joint angles with a mean absolute error of 6.1 degrees from the frozen features of vision-language models, significantly outperforming the text-based predictions (20.0 degrees).
- Fine-tuning the models using LoRA narrows this gap, suggesting a training objective deficit rather than a representational one.
- Training objectives determine accuracy more than architecture, as five diverse encoders converge to statistically equivalent geometric prediction performance despite low representational similarity.
- Autoregressive text generation damages geometric fidelity, but the damage originates in the generation process, not in language alignment.
- The authors identify a universal mid-network accuracy peak across all architectures, with attention heads in layers 18-22 carrying disproportionate geometric signal.

**Significance & Limitations**

This work has significant implications for the field of AI and machine learning. By revealing the untapped geometric knowledge within foundation models, the authors open up new avenues for developing AI systems that can seamlessly integrate and utilize continuous physical and spatial information. This could lead to breakthroughs in areas such as robotic manipulation, augmented reality, and embodied AI, where the ability to accurately represent and reason about the physical world is crucial.

One potential limitation of this study is the focus on a specific task of hand joint angle prediction. While this serves as a meaningful proxy for continuous geometric knowledge, the authors acknowledge that further research is needed to explore the models' understanding of other physical and spatial properties, such as object shapes, scene layouts, and dynamics. Additionally, the study does not delve into the underlying mechanisms that lead to the observed differences between visual and linguistic representations, leaving room for further investigation.
