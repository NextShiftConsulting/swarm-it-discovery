# Neural geometric representations of social memory for multi-individuals in medial prefrontal cortex

**RSCT Certification:** κ=0.550 (pending) | **RSN:** 0.37/0.32/0.31 | **Topics:** representation-learning

Neural Geometric Representations of Social Memory in the Medial Prefrontal Cortex

**Core Contribution**

This paper presents a novel approach to understanding how the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) encodes and maintains memories of multiple social identities over an extended period. The key finding is that mPFC neurons represent individual social identities through tuned geometric subspaces in the population activity, rather than through exclusive social-selective responses in individual neurons. This discovery provides crucial insights into the computational mechanisms underlying complex social memory in the brain.

The ability to recognize and remember specific individuals is a fundamental aspect of social cognition, allowing us to navigate the rich tapestry of our social world. By developing a sophisticated social identity discrimination task in mice and recording neuronal activity in the mPFC, the authors have uncovered a remarkable property of this brain region in encoding and retaining memories of multiple social identities over extended timescales, up to two weeks. This work represents a significant advancement in our understanding of the neural basis of social memory, with important implications for understanding social cognition and its dysfunction in various neuropsychiatric disorders.

**Technical Approach**

The researchers employed a novel experimental paradigm to investigate the neural representations of social identities in the mPFC. Mice were trained to discriminate between four conspecifics (fellow mice) in a social identity discrimination task, while the spiking activity of mPFC neurons was recorded using in vivo electrophysiology. This allowed the authors to directly observe how mPFC neurons encode and maintain the memories of multiple social identities over time.

Notably, the researchers used a cross-session alignment approach, combining linear regression and joint-dimensionality reduction techniques, to identify low-dimensional neural subspaces that differentially represented individual social identities across multiple days. This innovative approach enabled the researchers to track the dynamic changes in neural representations as the mice experienced different social interactions and learned new associations with the various individuals.

To further explore the relationship between social memory and valence (positive, negative, or neutral), the researchers conditioned the mice to associate specific individuals with rewarding, aversive, or neutral stimuli. They found that changes in social valence significantly increased the distance and angle between the neural subspaces representing different individuals, suggesting that the mPFC encodes both social identity and associated affective information.

**Key Results**

The key finding of this study is that mPFC neurons do not exhibit exclusive social-selective responses, but rather represent individual social identities through tuned geometric subspaces in the population activity. This encoding scheme allows the mPFC to maintain stable, long-term memories of multiple social identities, even in the face of changing social experiences and valence associations.

The authors also demonstrate that shifts in social valence, induced by conditioning, result in significant changes in the neural representations of individual social identities. Specifically, the distance and angle between the neural subspaces encoding different individuals increased, indicating a progressive recruitment of neurons that encode both identity and associated valence information.

**Significance & Limitations**

This study provides a computational framework for understanding the neural mechanisms underlying complex social memory. By revealing the geometric subspace representation of social identities in the mPFC, the authors have uncovered a novel principle of neural coding that may extend beyond the social domain. This work opens up new avenues for investigating the neural basis of social cognition and its disruption in various neurological and psychiatric disorders.

While this study offers valuable insights, it is important to consider potential limitations. The research was conducted in mice, and the generalizability of these findings to human social cognition remains to be established. Additionally, the study focused on the mPFC, but social memory likely involves the coordinated activity of multiple brain regions. Further research is needed to elucidate the broader neural network dynamics underlying social identity representations and their interactions with other cognitive and affective processes.
