# Layer by layer, module by module: Choose both for optimal OOD probing of ViT

**RSCT Certification:** κ=0.494 (pending) | **RSN:** 0.37/0.32/0.31 | **Topics:** representation-learning

Layer by Layer, Module by Module: Optimal OOD Probing of Vision Transformers

**Core Contribution**

This paper presents a comprehensive study on the behavior of intermediate layers in pretrained vision transformers (ViTs). The key finding is that distribution shift between the pretraining and downstream data is the primary driver of performance degradation in deeper ViT layers. Furthermore, the authors perform a fine-grained analysis at the module level, revealing that the standard approach of probing transformer block outputs is suboptimal. Instead, probing the activation within the feedforward network yields the best performance under significant distribution shift, while the normalized output of the multi-head self-attention module is optimal when the shift is weaker.

This work is important because it sheds light on the inner workings of ViTs, a powerful class of models that have shown impressive performance on a range of computer vision tasks. Understanding the behavior of intermediate representations and how they are affected by distributional shift is crucial for leveraging ViTs in real-world applications, where the training and deployment domains often differ. Additionally, the insights from this study can inform the development of more robust and generalizable ViT architectures and training procedures.

**Technical Approach**

The authors conduct extensive linear probing experiments on a diverse set of image classification benchmarks to analyze the performance of intermediate ViT layers. They explore two primary factors: (1) the choice of layer, ranging from the shallow to the deeper layers, and (2) the choice of module, focusing on the feedforward network (FFN) and the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) module within each transformer block.

The experiments are carried out on several pretrained ViT models, including ViT-Base, ViT-Large, and DeiT-Base, across a range of distribution shift scenarios. The authors consider both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) settings, using datasets such as ImageNet, Places365, and CIFAR-10/100 to simulate varying levels of shift.

To perform the fine-grained analysis at the module level, the authors extract the activations from the FFN and MHSA modules and probe them independently, in addition to the standard approach of probing the entire transformer block output.

**Key Results**

The study reveals several key findings:

1. Deeper ViT layers often underperform shallower layers in the OOD setting, contrary to the conventional wisdom that deeper representations are more discriminative.
2. The primary driver of this performance degradation is the distribution shift between the pretraining and downstream data, rather than the autoregressive pretraining as previously suggested.
3. Probing the activation within the FFN module yields the best OOD performance, especially under significant distribution shift. This outperforms the standard approach of probing the entire transformer block output.
4. When the distribution shift is weaker, probing the normalized output of the MHSA module provides the optimal performance.

These insights suggest that the choice of layer and module for probing ViTs should be carefully considered, depending on the specific characteristics of the downstream task and the degree of distribution shift.

**Significance & Limitations**

This work is significant because it challenges the prevailing notion that deeper ViT layers always yield more discriminative representations. By uncovering the impact of distribution shift, the authors provide a more nuanced understanding of the inner workings of ViTs and offer practical guidance for leveraging these models effectively.

The findings have important implications for the deployment of ViTs in real-world scenarios, where distribution shift is often unavoidable. The insights can inform the design of more robust ViT architectures and training procedures that are less sensitive to distributional changes.

Potential limitations of this study include the reliance on linear probing as the primary evaluation metric, which may not capture the full extent of the model's representational capacity. Additionally, the analysis is primarily focused on image classification tasks, and the applicability of the findings to other computer vision problems, such as object detection or segmentation, remains to be investigated.

Future research directions could explore the underlying mechanisms behind the observed performance differences between FFN and MHSA modules, as well as the generalization of these insights to other transformer-based models beyond ViTs.
